# Technical writing

Good technical writing prioritizes: useful information, specificity,
technical credibility, concrete examples, clarity without dumbing down, a
real point of view, information density, reader payoff, distinctive
phrasing, narrative momentum, sentence and paragraph variation.

Avoid synthetic patterns: generic scene-setting intros, "In today's rapidly
evolving..." openings, excessive section symmetry, repeated rhetorical
questions, fake quotations, inspirational-poster conclusions, empty
transitions, arbitrary lists of three, "Here's the thing", "It's not X,
it's Y", em dashes as a voice substitute, conclusions that merely
summarize.

Optimize for distinctive, specific, useful, credible — surprising when the
material earns it, appropriate to writer and audience. Never "sound human";
earn attention with substance. Prefer one concrete example over three
abstract paragraphs. Cut anything interchangeable with a thousand AI
articles.

## Information architecture: understanding over completeness

Fact completeness is not reader understanding. A complete inventory of
true statements, listed in no particular order, reads as compressed
documentation no matter how accurate each sentence is. Organize facts
around a conceptual hierarchy the reader can hold in their head:

1. why this matters (the reader payoff, first),
2. the core mental model (one idea, named plainly),
3. the important mechanics (how it works, in service of the model),
4. practical consequences (what to do differently),
5. limitations / tradeoffs (what it costs, where it breaks).

This is guidance, not a section template — never force all articles into
identical structures. But check the failure mode directly: if every
paragraph feels equally weighted, the strongest insight is buried, or a
reader could mistake the piece for reference docs, the hierarchy is
missing. Facts serve the idea; the idea is not decoration on the facts.

## Revising without losing density

When revising an accurate but flat draft, improve thesis, framing,
opening, structure, and momentum while preserving substantive technical
content. Cut only what is redundant, unsupported, or irrelevant to the
reader — never delete useful detail to manufacture engagement. A more
readable draft with fewer facts, dropped caveats, or vaguer guidance is
a regression, not an improvement.
